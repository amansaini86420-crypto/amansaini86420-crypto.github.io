Project 01 · Economic research

Literacy and the Growth Gap

Does higher literacy automatically lead to higher income and output, or do other factors matter?

Public dataExcelStatisticsKerala · West Bengal · Haryana · Maharashtra
95.3%Highest literacy: Kerala (2023-24)
₹3.26 LHighest income per head: Haryana
0.41Correlation, literacy vs income per head
0.95Correlation, industry share vs income per head
1

What problem was I solving?

Public debate often treats education as the route to prosperity: raise literacy and income follows. This project tests that claim against four states with very different profiles, and asks what else has to be in place for literacy to turn into output.

Questions the analysis answers

  • Which state has the highest literacy, and which has the highest income per head? Do they match?
  • How closely are literacy and income aligned across the four states?
  • Does high literacy come with high employment, or do jobs differ?
  • Which structural factor lines up most closely with income: industry or services?
  • Can literacy alone explain a state's economic output?
2

Twelve indicators for each of four states, gathered from public sources. Every value is logged in a Raw Data sheet with its source, year and a confidence rating, so each number can be traced back and checked.

Cleaning and checks

What I did

  • Moved values from the Raw Data sheet to a Clean Data sheet with one consistent row per state.
  • Averaged the four 2023-24 quarters to get one labour force participation rate per state.
  • Calculated real growth from the constant-price NSDP of 2022-23 and 2023-24.
  • Checked that the three sector shares add to 100 (they come to 100.0 or 100.01).

What I left out, and why

  • Employment rate (worker-population ratio) and manufacturing share could not be found for all four states, so they are excluded instead of estimated.
  • Several values come from secondary sources. They are flagged in the confidence column and should be checked against the original documents before citing.
  • Years differ across indicators. Each value keeps its own year.

Key insights

  • Highest literacy and highest income are different states. Kerala leads on literacy, Haryana on income per head.
  • Literacy and income are only moderately aligned (r = 0.41).
  • Kerala has the highest unemployment (7.0%) despite the highest literacy. Skills need jobs to turn into output.
  • Industry share lines up most closely with income (r = 0.95). Services share shows no clear link (r = -0.12).

Conclusion

Education is a foundation for development, but higher literacy does not automatically raise economic output. Jobs, industrialisation, productivity and the structure of the economy shape how well human capital turns into growth.

Limits

  • Four states only. The correlations are descriptive and cannot show cause.
  • Years differ across indicators, and some values need checking against the original RBI, PLFS and state documents.

Project 02 · Market research

Maruti Suzuki Showroom Sites, Mumbai

Which of Mumbai's 24 wards offers the best opportunity for a new Maruti Suzuki showroom, judged on demand, affordability, accessibility, competition and economics?

Public data + assumptionsSQLPythonStatisticsPower BI design
Status. Demand and affordability are measured from Census 2011. Cost, accessibility, the dealer list and all finance inputs are still assumed or unverified. The result is a shortlist for further evaluation, not a final recommendation.
12.9%Of Mumbai households owned a car in 2011
41,937Car-owning households in K/W (Andheri West), the most of any ward

1

What problem was I solving?

A new showroom is a long commitment. Its success depends on how many nearby households can afford a car, how many competitors are already there, what the site costs and how easily customers can reach it. This project scores each of Mumbai's 24 BMC wards on those factors, without assuming that wealthy areas will not buy a Maruti.

How the project was set up

  • Charter and hypotheses first. The question, hypotheses and a source register were written before any data was loaded.
  • Scoring and sensitivity designed in advance. The five components, their weights and the stress tests were fixed before any result existed, so weights could not be tuned to a preferred answer.
  • Every statement tagged. Each finding is marked as data, assumption or interpretation, and no number is quoted unless it came from a run on the real files.
2

A census structural proxy (literacy, female work, household size) was tested early and rejected as a stand-in for income. It is mechanically tied to household size, so it was replaced by the asset-based measures from the houselisting tables.

CheckClusters, regression and a finance test

The opportunity score

ComponentBase weightBuilt fromInput
Demand30%Car-owning householdsMeasured

Four alternative weight scenarios (A to D) were run beside the base case. The stress test then drew 3,000 random weight sets and shifted every assumed tier by -1, 0 or +1 in each run.

Other methods

  • K-Means on ownership variables. Two groups, silhouette 0.36. The earlier clusters on demographics alone were weak (silhouette 0.23).
  • OLS regression of car share on literacy, household size, SC/ST share and computer ownership. R-squared 0.85, adjusted 0.81, VIF 1.3 to 2.3, residuals pass the Shapiro test (p = 0.80) and Breusch-Pagan (p = 0.09).
  • Check of my own assumptions. My assumed affluence tiers correlated 0.85 (Spearman, p below 0.001) with measured car share.
  • Finance check. Yearly purchases by the car owners of each ward, against break-even sales at assumed costs.
  • Engineering. The analysis library has 9 passing unit tests, the SQL schema has 17 queries tested on a fixture, and run_all.py reruns the measured pipeline from the raw files.

Where the competition map has gaps

Leads for investigation, not conclusions.
  • No listed Maruti outlet: B, C, E, F/S, G/N, H/E, M/E and R/N.
  • One listed outlet for a large base: P/N (211,642 households), S, N and F/N.
  • K/W is contested: it already has 4 listed Maruti outlets.

Would an outlet pay for itself?

Not on the ward's own car owners. At assumed costs, no ward's own car-owning households support one outlet. In K/W even the high case would need 132% of its households' purchases. A real showroom also draws from neighbouring wards, first-time buyers, used cars and service, and its costs may be lower than assumed. So this is the bar that real catchment and rent data must clear, not a verdict.

Yearly purchases against break-even sales

Bar: assumed range of Maruti-bound purchases by the ward's own car-owning households (8 to 12% yearly purchase rate, 10 to 18% Maruti share). Black tick: sales needed to break even at assumed costs.

Key insights

  • Demand is concentrated. Only 12.9% of Mumbai households owned a car in 2011. K/W has the most car owners, and D, H/W and K/W have the highest ownership rates (25 to 30%).
  • Size and share point to different wards. D has the highest car share but a smaller base. K/W combines a high share with the largest base.
  • Wealthy does not mean empty. K/W is a large but contested market, with 4 listed outlets and the highest cost tier I assumed.
  • Assumed inputs can mislead. The assumption-only winner, R/N, fell to 9th when ownership was measured.
  • Households alone are a weak guide. The 10 largest wards hold 59.5% of households, but size says nothing about who can buy a car.
  • M/E is structurally different. It has the lowest literacy (83.4%), the lowest worker share (35.3%), one of the largest households (4.85 people) and the lowest car share (4.8%).

Project 03 · Customer analytics

Why Hotstar Customers Left in 2023

Which customers were most likely to churn, what drove it, and what could Hotstar have done to keep them?

Synthetic dataSQLPythonStatisticsPower BI design
Data note. The customer-level dataset is synthetic and was built for this analysis. The 2023 business context comes from public reporting. The patterns below show how the method works. They are not real Hotstar figures.
40.5%Overall churn (8,093 of 19,999)
0.82Churn model ROC-AUC
₹2.38 crRevenue at risk (churned customers × average annual revenue)
67.5%Of lost revenue came from Monthly plans
1

What problem was I solving?

In 2023 Disney+ Hotstar lost paying subscribers in every quarter. The business needs to know who left, why, and what to do about it. Losing the IPL is the obvious explanation, so it is treated here as one hypothesis to test, not the assumed answer.

Paid subscribers, millions

Disney's reported drop from April to June 2023 was 12.5 million, a 24% fall in one quarter.
Disney earnings coverage (Business Today, TechCrunch, Inc42, Telecomtalk). Sep and Dec 2022 follow from the reported quarterly changes.

What changed in 2023

Public context, not part of the dataset.
  • IPL rights. Viacom18 (JioCinema) won the 2023-27 digital rights and streamed the 2023 season free.
  • Premium content. HBO, Max and Warner titles moved to JioCinema.
  • Separate event. The Reliance-Disney joint venture was announced in 2024, so it is not part of the 2023 story.

Business questions

  • What was the overall churn, and which customer segments churned most?
  • Did plan type, engagement, inactivity, content or complaints drive churn?
  • Was IPL dependency linked to churn, and how strongly compared with other factors?
  • Where is the revenue at risk, and can churn be predicted early enough to act?
2

How did I collect and clean the data?

A real customer file was not available, so I generated a synthetic one with a seeded Python script. It has about 20,000 customers active at some point in 2023 (62% existing on 1 January, 38% joined during the year) and 27 columns. Messy data was built in on purpose, so the cleaning work is real. Churn means a customer cancelled or did not renew in 2023, and that definition never changes.

20,380 raw rows became 19,999 clean customers

Nothing was removed without a reason. Only one row was dropped, because it could not be recovered.

How did I analyse it?

CleanPython cleaning script and cleaning log
SQL6-table model, 16 queries with CTEs and window functions
ExploreChurn by plan, age, engagement, content and more
TestChi-square, Mann-Whitney, controlled comparisons
ModelLogistic regression and decision tree

Methods and why I chose them

  • Medians and log scales for watch time. It is right-skewed (mean 15.7 hours, median 12.9).
  • Effect size over p-values. With 20,000 rows almost every test is significant, so the size of the gap matters more.
  • Controlled comparisons. To test the IPL link I compared high and low IPL dependency within the same engagement level and plan.
  • No leakage in the model. Tenure is measured at the start of 2023, not at the churn date, which would reveal the outcome.
  • Cross-validated risk scores. Each customer is scored by a model that never saw them.

Key insights

  • Plan and inactivity matter most. Monthly plans churn at 48.4% against 23.2% on Annual, and a customer idle for 8 to 14 days is already at 53%.
  • IPL dependency adds risk on top of other factors, but it is one factor among several. Only 12.0% of stated churn reasons were the IPL, while 22.8% named switching to JioCinema or free options and 22.1% named price.
  • Newer customers were retained worse. Six-month retention was 72.2% for 2021 starters, 70.1% for 2022 and 60.4% for 2023 joiners.
  • Service problems show a clear pattern. Churn rises from 38.4% with no complaints to 60.5% with three or more.

Recommendations

  • Win back idle users at day 7 to 10. After 30 days most have gone.
  • Move Monthly users to Quarterly or Annual at a usage milestone, and turn on auto-renew. No blanket discounts.
  • Keep sports-heavy users with other live sport, highlights and tournament passes.
  • Fix renewal friction and complaints. Payment failures raise churn to 53%.
  • Send a weekly scored list to CRM. 1,231 high-risk customers who stayed carry ₹39 lakh of annual revenue.

Limits

  • Synthetic data. Effect sizes reflect how it was generated and need confirming on real data.
  • Association is not causation. Price sensitivity and plan commitment cannot be separated here.
  • Inactivity near exit is partly a symptom. A production model needs features from a window before renewal.

ShopSphere Funnel Analytics

Where do users drop off between visit and purchase, which variables explain it, and what should the business fix first?

What problem was I solving?

An online store gets traffic, but only a small share of visitors buy. Marketing spend and product work are wasted if the biggest leak is not found first. This project measures every step from visit to order, finds where users drop off and tests which variables explain it.

Questions the analysis answers

  • At which step do most users leave, and where do engaged users leak?
  • Do device and traffic channel change conversion, and at which step?
  • Which variables are linked to lower conversion or lower repeat purchase?
  • What should the business fix first, and how much could it be worth?

How did I analyse it?

  • Funnel analysis. Conversion and drop-off at every step, overall and by device and channel.
  • Relationship analysis. Correlation and driver charts for page speed, shipping fees, payment failures and delivery time against conversion and repeat purchase.
  • Retention. 60-day repeat rate, split by delivery time.
  • What-if scenarios. Directional sizing of each fix, so recommendations can be ranked by likely impact.
  • Dashboard design. A two-page Power BI layout with DAX measures.

What insights did the analysis uncover?

Where users drop off

  • Biggest loss overall: visit to signup. 79% of visitors leave here.
  • Biggest leak among engaged users: product view to checkout. 63% drop.

Share of users who leave at each step

Visit to signup is the biggest loss. Among engaged users, product view to checkout is the leak.

Share of traffic by device

Mobile carries most of the traffic, which is why its checkout matters.

Mobile is the problem

Mobile is 61% of traffic but converts at about half the desktop rate, and its checkouts fail on payment more than twice as often.

Signup rate by channel

Channels differ at signup but behave almost the same afterwards.

Also associated with lower conversion

  • Slow pages, shipping fees shown late and failed payments.
  • Slower delivery is associated with a lower repeat purchase rate.

Recommendations

  • Fix the mobile checkout. Reach rate is 31% on mobile against 46% on desktop.
  • Test guest checkout or delayed signup, and cut mobile payment failures.
  • Show shipping cost early and test a lower free-shipping threshold.
  • Improve mobile page speed, and shift budget from Social toward Email and Direct.
  • Keep delivery within 3 days to protect repeat purchase.

Limits

  • Impact estimates are directional what-if sizing on synthetic data and would need A/B tests before anyone acts on them.
